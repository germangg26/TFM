\chapter{Deployment and business analysis}
\label{cap:negocio}

This chapter closes the pipeline with its applied dimension: an example deployment of the system as a service and a return-on-investment (ROI) analysis that translates the propensity predictions into a business decision.

\section{Deployment}

As a demonstration of productisation, an example REST API (FastAPI) was implemented to serve the recommendations: for a known user it returns its precomputed top-5 in \textit{batch}; for a new user it applies the cold-start pipeline (\textit{features} $\rightarrow$ demographic cluster $\rightarrow$ cluster affinities); and it falls back to popularity as a last resort. The API also exposes a \texttt{/propensity} \textit{endpoint} that, from the flat M2 model, returns for a user or a profile the click probability broken down by sector and by product (independent \textit{one-vs-rest} probabilities, corrected to the true prior), which is the operational way of deciding what to send to whom. It is not a production service, but an illustration of the inference flow.

\subsection{API specification}

The API exposes five \textit{endpoints} (Table~\ref{tab:api}). The inputs to the \texttt{POST} methods are a JSON object with the user profile, whose categorical fields are entered as readable text (e.g.\ \texttt{mun\_type="Ciudad"}); the API internally performs the numerical encoding that the model expects, guaranteeing the same variable order as in training. All fields are optional and have a default value, and out-of-domain inputs are automatically rejected with a 422 error.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Endpoints of the example API}
\label{tab:api}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Method and path} & \textbf{Input} & \textbf{Output} \\
\midrule
\texttt{GET /health}                 & ---            & status and no.\ of artefacts \\
\texttt{GET /recommend/\{user\_id\}}  & user id        & top-5 of cluster categories \\
\texttt{POST /recommend/coldstart}    & profile (JSON) & top-5 via demographic cluster \\
\texttt{GET /propensity/\{user\_id\}} & user id        & click prob.\ by sector and product \\
\texttt{POST /propensity}             & profile (JSON) & click prob.\ by sector and product \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The \texttt{/propensity} \textit{endpoint} returns, for a user or profile, the click probability of each of the 13 sectors and the 25 product categories (each product accompanied by its sector), already corrected to the true \textit{prior} ($\approx 2$\,\%). These are independent probabilities (\textit{one-vs-rest}): each one answers ``what is the click probability if I send this?'' and they do not sum to one.

\subsection{Worked example: input and output}

To make the input and output concrete, consider a new user (cold start). The request body is a JSON object with the demographic profile; categorical fields are given as readable text and any field that is omitted takes its default value. For instance, a \texttt{POST /propensity} call with the following input:

\begin{lstlisting}[language={},numbers=none,basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize,caption={Request --- \texttt{POST /propensity} (input profile)},captionpos=b]
{
  "age": 38,
  "gender": "M",
  "civil_status": "casado",
  "tiene_coche": true,
  "ipa_class": "Media-alta",
  "mun_type": "Ciudad"
}
\end{lstlisting}

returns the click probability broken down by sector and by product (truncated here to the top entries). The probabilities are \textit{one-vs-rest} and corrected to the true prior, so they do not add up to one:

\begin{lstlisting}[language={},numbers=none,basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize,caption={Response --- \texttt{POST /propensity} (output, abridged)},captionpos=b]
{
  "source": "plano_coldstart",
  "user_id": null,
  "sectores": [
    {"sector": "motor",   "p_click": 0.041},
    {"sector": "seguros", "p_click": 0.028},
    {"sector": "teleco",  "p_click": 0.021}
  ],
  "productos": [
    {"producto": "car_leasing",   "sector": "motor",  "p_click": 0.046},
    {"producto": "car_insurance", "sector": "motor",  "p_click": 0.039},
    {"producto": "mobile_plan",   "sector": "teleco", "p_click": 0.022}
  ]
}
\end{lstlisting}

These probabilities are low in absolute terms because they are expressed at the true production prior ($\approx 2$\,\%): a value such as $0{,}046$ does not indicate a low propensity but roughly twice the average click rate, i.e.\ an above-average prospect. This is the expected scale once the sampling bias is corrected, and it is exactly the value on which the ROI decision rule ($p_\text{real}\cdot\text{CPL}\ge$ marginal cost) operates.

The same profile sent to \texttt{POST /recommend/coldstart} returns, instead, the top-5 product categories of the user's demographic cluster:

\begin{lstlisting}[language={},numbers=none,basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize,caption={Response --- \texttt{POST /recommend/coldstart} (output)},captionpos=b]
{
  "recommendations": ["car_insurance", "health_insurance", "energy_plan",
                      "funeral_insurance", "car_leasing"],
  "source": "cold_start_cluster",
  "demo_cluster": 3
}
\end{lstlisting}

For a \emph{known} user, the \texttt{GET} variants take the \texttt{user\_id} in the path instead of a JSON body and return the same structures, but with \texttt{"source": "cluster\_m1"} (the recommender uses the user's behavioural segment, reported in \texttt{cluster\_id}) and \texttt{"source": "plano\_usuario"} for the propensity breakdown.

\section{Return-on-investment (ROI) analysis}
\label{sec:roi}

To evaluate the business value, we compare the ROI of the campaign recorded in the database
(``send to all'') against our model (``prioritise''). The CPL is interpreted as the profit
left by each click; the profit of a product is (clicks $\times$ CPL). The cost of sending an email is
analysed under three structures, all per email sent: \emph{fixed} (a value equal for all
products, 1\,\euro), \emph{variable} (a percentage of the product's CPL; 20\,\%, 30\,\% and
40\,\% are compared) and \emph{mixed} (a fixed base of 0,50\,\euro\ plus 15\,\% of the CPL). The model sends an email only
if its expected profit ($p_\text{model}\times\text{CPL}$) exceeds its marginal cost; in the variable case
this rule is exactly equivalent to $p_\text{model}\ge\text{percentage}$. Table~\ref{tab:roi_producto} reports the aggregate ROI by cost structure.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Aggregate ROI: DB campaign vs.\ prioritisation model, by cost-per-email structure}
\label{tab:roi_producto}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Cost structure} & \textbf{DB ROI} & \textbf{Model ROI} & \textbf{Improvement} & \textbf{Emails} & \textbf{Prod.\ improve} \\
\midrule
Fixed (1\,\euro/email)        &    101\% & \textbf{148\%} & \textbf{+47\,pts} & 72,0\% & 21/25 \\
Variable 20\,\% CPL           &     18\% & \textbf{67\%}  & \textbf{+49\,pts} & 48,2\% & 22/25 \\
Variable 30\,\% CPL           & $-$21\%  & \textbf{37\%}  & \textbf{+58\,pts} & 24,3\% & 21/25 \\
Variable 40\,\% CPL           & $-$41\%  & \textbf{21\%}  & \textbf{+62\,pts} & 12,2\% & 19/25 \\
Mixed (0,50\,\euro\ + 15\,\% CPL) & 13\% & \textbf{65\%}  & \textbf{+52\,pts} & 41,7\% & 21/25 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Unlike the purely fixed structure \emph{per product} (where sending to all would be optimal because
the cost does not depend on volume), since the marginal cost is per email, prioritisation adds value in
the three structures: the model stops sending the emails whose expected profit does not cover their cost and discards
loss-making products (low CPL and little propensity, e.g.\ \texttt{life\_insurance}, \texttt{sweepstakes},
\texttt{loan}). The more expensive the email (high percentages of the CPL), the more selective the model becomes
(from 72\,\% of sends with fixed cost to 12\,\% with 40\,\% of the CPL). The aggregate ROI for every cost structure is reported in Table~\ref{tab:roi_producto}.


The \emph{fixed} case (1\,\euro/email) improves the ROI from 101\,\% to 148\,\% by sending to the 72\,\% with the highest
propensity: it filters out the $\approx$28\,\% of sends whose expected profit does not cover the cost. In the
\emph{variable} case, the rule $p_\text{model}\ge\text{percentage}$ is stricter the higher the
percentage: sending to all turns out to be \emph{loss-making} from 30\,\% onwards (negative ROI), because the cost per
email is high relative to the click rate of the \emph{sample} ($\approx$24\,\%); the model rescues it to
positive in all cases (from $-$21\,\% to $+$37\,\% with 30\,\%; from $-$41\,\% to $+$21\,\% with
40\,\%), at the cost of sending fewer and fewer. The \emph{mixed} case improves from 13\,\% to 65\,\%.

\begin{hallazgo}
Under a marginal cost per email, the prioritisation model improves the ROI in the three
cost structures (e.g.\ 101\,\%\,$\to$\,148\,\% in the fixed case): it filters out the sends whose expected profit
does not cover their cost and rescues to positive even the scenarios where sending to all is
loss-making (variable $\geq$30\,\%). The degree of filtering depends on the cost per email: from the $\approx$28\,\% of
sends discarded with fixed cost to $\approx$88\,\% with 40\,\% of the CPL. The choice of operating point
depends on the business objective: maximising \emph{efficiency} (\euro\ per click) or \emph{reach} (total
clicks).
\end{hallazgo}

\paragraph{Note: propensity, not incrementality.} The model estimates the \emph{propensity} (click probability), not the \emph{incrementality} or \textit{uplift} (the \emph{causal} effect of the send): the ROI attributes to the email all the clicks of the prioritised users, even though some would have occurred anyway without receiving it. Quantifying that causal effect would require a control group (comparable users not impacted), which these data (only recorded sends) do not allow us to build. The nuance affects the \emph{absolute} reading of the figures, not the relative conclusion: since ``send to all'' and ``prioritise'' are evaluated under the same assumption, the comparison between the two is fair and the model improves the ROI in all scenarios. The estimation of incrementality is left as a line of future work.

\subsection{ROI at production scale}

The previous figures are computed on the balanced evaluation sample (click rate $\approx$24\,\%); they therefore compare, \emph{in relative terms}, ``send to all'' against ``prioritise''. To estimate the \emph{absolute} production ROI, each send is reweighted to the true prior ($\approx$2\,\%) and the decision is made with the \emph{corrected} probability (send if $p_\text{real}\cdot\text{CPL}\ge$ marginal cost). The result (Table~\ref{tab:roi_prod}) is illustrative.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{ROI at production scale (true prior $\approx$2\,\%): DB campaign vs.\ model}
\label{tab:roi_prod}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Cost structure} & \textbf{ROI ``send to all''} & \textbf{Model ROI} & \textbf{Emails sent} \\
\midrule
Fixed (1\,\euro/email)        & $-$83\% & \textbf{$-$1\%} & 0,2\% \\
Variable 20\,\% CPL           & $-$90\% & $-$51\%        & $\approx$0\% \\
Variable 30\,\% CPL           & $-$93\% & $-$100\%       & $\approx$0\% \\
Variable 40\,\% CPL           & $-$95\% & ---            & 0\% \\
Mixed (0,50\,\euro\ + 15\,\% CPL) & $-$91\% & $-$37\%   & $\approx$0\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

At a true click rate of 2\,\%, sending to all loses money in every structure ($-$83\,\% to $-$95\,\%): with an expected profit per email of $0{,}02\times8{,}5\approx0{,}17$\,\euro\ against costs of 1\,\euro\ or more, the channel is structurally loss-making. Not even the most favourable case (low fixed cost) becomes profitable: the model takes it from $-$83\,\% to barely the break-even point ($\approx-1$\,\%), sending only to the 0,2\,\% most propense. Under variable cost (a percentage of the CPL) the rule requires $p_\text{real}\ge$ percentage (20--40\,\%), unattainable when the 90th percentile of $p_\text{real}$ is 4,7\,\%; barely anything is sent and the channel remains loss-making. The business reading is clear: at real scale, targeting is indispensable but not sufficient, and what dominates profitability is the unit economics (cost per email against CPL). For the objective of this work, demonstrating the \emph{methodology} of an ROI-oriented project, this result is as valuable as a positive one: the process quantifies the binding economic constraint and avoids recommending a loss-making campaign, which is in itself a well-founded business decision. That the pipeline exposes this fact instead of hiding it is, precisely, part of its value.
