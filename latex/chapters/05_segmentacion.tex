\chapter{Module M0/M1 — Customer segmentation}
\label{cap:segmentacion}

This chapter brings together the two levels of unsupervised user segmentation, presenting for each its method and its results. The \textbf{demographic} segmentation (M0) assigns a profile to new users with no history; the \textbf{behavioural} segmentation (M1) groups active users by their interaction pattern.

\section{M0 — Demographic segmentation (cold start)}

\subsection{Method}

Demographic segmentation aims to assign an expected behaviour profile to new users for whom no interaction history exists (cold start). It is applied to the 35.405 users of the dataset using ten profile variables: age \emph{categorised} into bands (\texttt{age\_cat}, the same as in M2), gender, employment status, marital status, vehicle ownership, household size, number of rooms, purchasing power index (IPA), municipality type and distance to the nearest urban centre. The categorical variables were ordinally encoded according to their semantic meaning before applying standard scaling.

\paragraph{Selection of the embedding space.} Three representation spaces were compared for the subsequent clustering by means of the silhouette coefficient with $k=2$ and $n=5.000$ samples: (1) the original standardised space (10 dimensions); (2) a PCA reduction down to 80\,\% of the explained variance (8 components); and (3) a non-linear embedding by means of an autoencoder (4 latent dimensions). The autoencoder has the architecture:
\[
10 \xrightarrow{\text{Dense}(64)} \xrightarrow{\text{BN}} \xrightarrow{\text{Dense}(32)} \xrightarrow{\text{Dense}(4)} \xrightarrow{\text{Dense}(32)} \xrightarrow{\text{BN}} \xrightarrow{\text{Dense}(64)} \xrightarrow{\text{Dense}(10)}
\]
(where BN denotes batch normalisation) and was trained with MSE loss, the Adam optimiser, batch size 256, validation split 0,1 and early stopping (patience 10 epochs). The autoencoder obtained the highest silhouette and was selected as the clustering space (results below). It was chosen over PCA for its capacity to capture \emph{non-linear} relationships between the profile variables, which a linear reduction does not capture~\cite{hinton2006}. Nevertheless, it should be noted that, since the demographic space is practically a continuum (as the $k$ stability diagnostic shows later), \textbf{PCA would have been a simpler alternative with comparable results}; the autoencoder provides a modest improvement in cohesion at the cost of greater complexity.

It is worth clarifying why the silhouette is indeed useful here to choose the \emph{space} but not to choose the \emph{number of clusters}: to compare spaces a common $k=2$ is fixed and one measures which separates better with that common $k$ (a \emph{relative} and fair comparison); by contrast, when sweeping $k$ the silhouette grows monotonically and its maximum does not indicate a natural number of groups, so for that second decision one resorts to stability (ARI).

\paragraph{Selection of $k$.} KMeans was evaluated for $k \in \{2,\ldots,12\}$ with four metrics: silhouette and Calinski-Harabasz (to maximise), Davies-Bouldin (to minimise) and inertia (elbow). As will be seen, the silhouette and the Calinski-Harabasz grow \emph{monotonically} with $k$ (with no interior maximum), which indicates that the demographic space is practically a continuum with no natural number of groups. The choice of $k$ was therefore based on the \textbf{stability} of the assignments by means of a resampling diagnostic (\textit{Adjusted Rand Index}, ARI~\cite{hubert1985}). As a reference, HDBSCAN was also run (search over \texttt{min\_cluster\_size}); its best configuration produced 18 clusters with 5,5\,\% of users unassigned (noise). KMeans was selected for its \textbf{full coverage} (0\,\% noise) and a more manageable number of groups.

\subsection{Results}

The autoencoder space (4 latent dimensions) outperformed PCA and the original in clustering cohesion measured by silhouette (0,344 versus 0,142 and 0,129 at $k=2$). KMeans with $k=10$ was selected on that space. The 10 clusters present interpretable differences: the largest ($n\approx4.900$; 13,8\,\%) groups the modal profile, whereas the minority ones ($n\approx1.500$) capture profiles of large dwellings or small municipalities. Table~\ref{tab:m0_space} summarises the silhouette of the three candidate spaces, and Figure~\ref{fig:umap_m0} shows the UMAP projection of the clusters.

\paragraph{k stability diagnostic.} The pointwise metrics do not fix an optimal $k$: the silhouette grows monotonically with $k$ (from 0,35 at $k=2$ to 0,46 at $k=12$), a sign that the demographic space is practically a \emph{continuum} with no well-separated natural groups; its maximum coincides with the upper bound of the search range and is not a reliable criterion. To decide rigorously, the \textbf{stability} of the clustering was evaluated by repeating KMeans 15 times on 80\,\% subsamples and measuring the ARI between resamplings, which quantifies how consistent the assignments are. Figure~\ref{fig:kstability} summarises the sweep. The ARI remains \textbf{very high across the whole range} ($0{,}96$--$1{,}0$): the clustering is stable for almost any $k$, so stability alone does not single out a unique value ($k=10$ obtains ARI $0{,}984$, very close to the maximum, which is at $k=11$ with $0{,}998$). \textbf{$k=10$ is adopted}: within that high-stability plateau it is a value that combines good cohesion (silhouette $\approx0{,}43$), an interpretable granularity and coherence with the rest of the pipeline. This value propagates to the dependent modules (propensity M2, cold start of M3 and the inverse model).

\section{M1 — Behavioural segmentation}

\subsection{Method}

The behavioural segmentation (M1) applies the same framework to a feature space that combines the interaction history with the user attributes. 46 variables are built in three blocks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Activity} (7 variables): number of events, clicks, opens, products, sectors, recency and click rate. The count variables are transformed with $\log(1+x)$ to reduce the skewness of the distribution.
    \item \textbf{Sector} (18 variables): event frequency and click rate per sector (9 sectors).
    \item \textbf{Product} (21 variables): event frequency per product category (top 20 + \textit{other}).
\end{itemize}
The dimensionality reduction was carried out with PCA, fixing 26 components (explained variance $\geq 90$\,\%). On the PCA space, KMeans ($k \in \{4,\ldots,12\}$), GMM ($k \in \{3,\ldots,9\}$) and HDBSCAN were evaluated, with the additional requirement that no cluster exceed 60\,\% of the base.

\subsection{Results}

KMeans with $k=12$ was selected (silhouette 0,347) over GMM ($k=9$, silhouette 0,249) and HDBSCAN (0,210; excluded due to 46\,\% of \textit{outliers}). The distribution of the segments reflects the skewness of the dataset: segment 3 groups 39,1\,\% of the users ($n=13.858$) with a \textit{click rate} of 0,01 (a single open with no click on insurance); at the opposite extreme, segments 1 and 2 concentrate high-activity users (\textit{click rates} 0,95 and 0,99) but only 8,3\,\% of the base. Table~\ref{tab:m1_algos} summarises this comparison. This segmentation is the one used by the recommender (Chapter~\ref{cap:recomendador}).
