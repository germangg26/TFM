\chapter{Module M3 — Cluster-level recommender}
\label{cap:recomendador}

\section{Method}

The recommender operates at the cluster level: the ranking of categories is computed per M1 behavioural segment (Chapter~\ref{cap:segmentacion}) and served to all of its members (a user obtains the recommendations of their cluster). This decision stems from the sparsity of the history. To justify it, we start from a hybrid scheme that combines two behavioural signals to score each (user, product category) pair:
\begin{equation}
    s(u, p) = \alpha \cdot \hat{p}_\text{ind}(u, p) + (1-\alpha) \cdot a(s_u, p)
    \label{eq:hybrid}
\end{equation}
where $a(s_u, p)$ is the affinity (normalised click rate) of the user's behavioural segment $s_u$ towards category $p$, and $\hat{p}_\text{ind}(u, p)$ is the historical click rate of the user themselves in that category. The individual component is computed solely from the training events (the M2 out-of-fold scores, which mixed information from the whole dataset and would have contaminated the temporal evaluation, are not reused).

The weight $\alpha$ is tuned on a validation set (not on the test set). With the current data the optimum is $\alpha=0$: given the sparsity (median of one event per user), the individual history contributes nothing and the useful signal is the segment affinity. The recommender therefore dispenses with the individual term and serves the cluster ranking directly. For new users without history (cold start) the affinity of the M0 demographic cluster is used.

\subsection*{Leakage-free evaluation}

Since the M1 segment is built from the entire history, evaluating it on a temporal split would contaminate the test (cluster membership would ``see'' the future). For this reason, \emph{for evaluation purposes}, the segment is reconstructed using only the train events (replicating the M1 pipeline: \texttt{log1p}\,$\rightarrow$\,standardisation\,$\rightarrow$\,PCA\,$\rightarrow$\,KMeans, $k{=}12$), and the affinity is also estimated using train data only; in service the segment is used with the full history (a legitimate use, not leakage). The evaluation uses a three-way temporal partition (train / validation / test, 70/10/20 by date). The metrics are Hit Rate@$K$, NDCG@$K$ and MAP@$K$ (Section~\ref{sec:metricas}), compared against two baselines (global popularity and demographic cluster). The statistical contrast combines the \textbf{Friedman} test (overall difference on the per-user NDCG@10), pairwise \textbf{Wilcoxon} post-hoc and \textbf{McNemar} (on the HR@5 hits), with Holm correction, effect sizes (Kendall's W and rank-biserial) and bootstrap confidence intervals.

Each method orders the categories by its own criterion (the recommender by the segment's \emph{click rate} and the baselines by the \emph{number of clicks}). Since the ranking metrics (HR@$K$, NDCG, MAP) depend solely on the \emph{order} of the categories and not on their scale, the way each signal is normalised does not affect the comparison.

\section{Results}

The recommender generates the top-5 categories at the cluster level. Table~\ref{tab:m3_results} and Figure~\ref{fig:m3_recommender} compare the recommender (leakage-free) with the baselines over 1.573 users with a click in test.

Once the leakage has been removed, the cluster-level recommender does not beat popularity on any metric. Popularity achieves a better HR@5 (0,629 vs.\ 0,586; McNemar test $p_{\text{Holm}}=3{,}4\times10^{-8}$, the baseline wins in 102 users against 35), and also a better HR@10, NDCG@10 and MAP@10. The Friedman test on the NDCG@10 confirms that there are differences between the three methods ($\chi^2=142$, $p\approx10^{-31}$), although its overall effect size is small (Kendall's W $=0{,}05$), because two of the three methods are almost identical. The Wilcoxon post-hoc with Holm correction makes this precise: popularity and demographic cluster are equivalent to each other ($p_{\text{Holm}}=0{,}31$; rank-biserial effect size $|r|=0{,}06$, negligible), whereas both beat the cluster-level recommender with a large effect size ($p_{\text{Holm}}<10^{-22}$; $|r|=0{,}65$ and $0{,}54$). The inferiority of the recommender is therefore not merely statistically significant: it is of considerable magnitude. The advantage of the behavioural recommender observed with the \emph{contaminated} segment (HR@5 of 0,687) was a leakage artefact (an inflation of $\approx0{,}10$ in HR@5).

This reading is consistent with the rest of the work: with a reduced catalogue (25 categories) and highly concentrated clicks, popularity is a very hard baseline to beat, and the segment signal adds no predictive power over it. The value of the cluster-level recommender is operational (interpretable segments, a homogeneous and stable ranking per group, and a natural cold-start solution), not one of ranking improvement over popularity.
