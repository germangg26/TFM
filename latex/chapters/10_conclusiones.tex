\section{Discussion}

The previous modules share a methodological lesson. The initial design treated \emph{propensity estimation} and \emph{recommendation} as a single problem and assumed, without testing it, that the user's demographic profile would be the determining variable. Separating the two problems and requiring that the evidence, rather than the starting intuition, decide which signal matters in each one changed the diagnosis. Which signal ultimately proves useful for each task is detailed in the following section; what is relevant here is that this question was answered by measuring, not by assuming.

The second lesson is that the way one validates can weigh more heavily than the choice of model. The data leakage in the propensity module did not stem from a flaw in the algorithm, but from a validation scheme that left the same user in both training and test; correcting it brought the apparent performance of the demographic model down to its realistic value. Something similar occurred with the recommender: rebuilding the segment using only training data removed an advantage that was an artefact. Comparing each model against trivial \textit{baselines} and backing every claim with paired tests (bootstrap, McNemar, Wilcoxon) and correction for multiple comparisons avoided accepting non-existent improvements as genuine.

The third lesson is that, in this problem, what most limits performance is not the modelling but the data. The user's demographic variables are synthetic, sampled from aggregate distributions, and provide little individual signal; this ceiling bounds what any algorithm can achieve and conditions the reading of all the modules.

\begin{hallazgo}
\textbf{Central conclusion.} The useful signal for \emph{propensity} resides in the product content
(sector, category and subject-line text); the useful signal for \emph{recommendation} resides in the user's
\emph{behaviour}, not in their demographic profile. The synthetic demographic variables set the performance
ceiling, so the value of this work lies in the methodology.
\end{hallazgo}

\section{Main conclusions}

This work has developed a comprehensive machine-learning \textit{framework} for optimising email marketing campaigns in the Spanish market, covering the full cycle from raw data ingestion through to personalised recommendation, demand forecasting and an example deployment. Beyond the modules, the main contribution is methodological: a reproducible pipeline that takes every decision in a justified manner (leakage-free validation, comparison against trivial \textit{baselines} before adopting a model, correction of sampling bias and statistical testing) and that distinguishes with evidence which signal is useful for each problem, which model deserves to be deployed and at what cost. Its value does not reside in a metric of maximum accuracy, but in a disciplined process that also reports what does \emph{not} work. Read in this way, the work functions as a guide for developing a data project aimed at maximising ROI: each stage shows what to build, how to validate it and how to ground the decision to deploy or not, including the decision \emph{not} to launch a campaign when the economics of the channel do not justify it.

Of the four falsifiable hypotheses set out in the introduction, \textbf{H1} is \emph{confirmed} (the product variables significantly improve propensity, $p\approx0$) and the other three are \emph{rejected}: H2 (the ablation study shows that the synthetic demographic variables provide no predictive power), H3 (the cluster-level recommender does not outperform popularity) and H4 (Prophet does not improve on the naïve baselines). Three rejected hypotheses are not a failure: they delimit with evidence where the useful signal of the problem lies, and where it does not.

Propensity and recommendation are distinct problems, and the useful signal differs according to the objective. To estimate the \emph{propensity} to click, the \emph{product} variables (the sector, the category and, above all, the email subject-line text encoded via \textit{embeddings}) contribute in a statistically significant manner: the model with product variables (PR-AUC 0,402) outperforms both the user-features-only model and the popularity and per-category click-rate baselines (see Chapter~\ref{cap:propension}), which confirms that it does not merely memorise the average propensity of each category, but exploits the subject-line text. A comparison of five algorithms and two architectures (flat versus hierarchical) confirms that the flat model outperforms the hierarchical one across all algorithms and that the neural network adds nothing on tabular data with a weak signal; the performance ceiling ($\approx0{,}40$ PR-AUC) is set by the data, not the algorithm. For \emph{recommendation}, by contrast, what discriminates is the user's \emph{behaviour} (their segment), not their demographic profile.

Validation changes the conclusions. The detection and correction of a data leakage in M2 (the use of per-event cross-validation instead of grouping by user) revealed that the real performance of the demographic model (AUC-ROC 0,563) was far lower than initially estimated (0,695). This finding constitutes a methodological contribution: it illustrates how an inadequate validation choice can misleadingly inflate the metrics in problems where the features are stable attributes of the user.

The cluster-level recommender provides operational, not predictive, value. It serves its recommendations \emph{at the cluster level} (behavioural segment M1). When evaluated without leakage, rebuilding the segment using only training data, it \emph{does not outperform} popularity on any metric (HR@5 0,586 against 0,629 in favour of popularity), a result confirmed by a Friedman test followed by Wilcoxon with Holm correction; the apparent advantage of the contaminated segment was a temporal-leakage artefact. Something analogous occurs with the demographic cluster from M0: it is the variable most \emph{used} by M2 (3rd by number of \textit{splits}), but the ablation study demonstrates that its predictive contribution is nil, since dispensing with it and the rest of the user variables does not reduce the PR-AUC. The value of both is therefore \emph{operational}: interpretable segments, homogeneous \textit{ranking} per group and a natural cold-start solution, assigning to each new user the affinities of their group. They do not improve the \textit{ranking} over a popularity that, with a catalogue of 25 categories and concentrated clicks, proves a very strong \textit{baseline}.

Module M4 produces predictions in the range 259--452 clicks/week for January--February 2026, with wide confidence intervals consistent with an active history of only 22 weeks. A re-audit by means of a 1-step rolling \textit{backtest} further reveals that Prophet does not outperform naïve baselines (naïve and moving average): its error is larger (MAE 750 against 502) although, with only 14 origins and the Harvey-Leybourne-Newbold small-sample correction, the difference does not reach significance at 5\% (Diebold-Mariano $p\approx0{,}06$), so the evidence is suggestive, not conclusive. On a short and volatile series, ``next week will be like the last'' matches or outperforms Prophet, a negative result that calls into question its added value in this context.

For users with a history, a \emph{warm} model that adds their past behaviour improves propensity in a notable and consistent manner (PR-AUC $0{,}83\,\to\,0{,}94$, with improvement across all five folds of a temporal cross-validation), compared with the \emph{cold} demographic model valid for new users. The probabilities of the flat model are reasonably well calibrated (\textit{Brier} 0,167 on the training scale). The flat propensity model is exposed in the example API through a \texttt{/propensity} \textit{endpoint} that, given a known user or a new profile, returns the click probability broken down by sector and by product (corrected to the real prior).

The economic analysis, interpreting the CPL as profit per click, shows that, under a marginal cost per email, prioritising the sends with the model improves ROI across the three cost structures analysed (fixed, variable and mixed; for example, from 101\,\% to 148\,\% in the fixed case), discarding the sends whose expected profit does not cover their cost and rescuing into positive territory even the scenarios where sending to everyone is loss-making. The optimal operating point depends on the business objective (efficiency versus reach). That said, this comparison is made on the balanced sample; when ROI is recomputed at production scale (reweighting to the real 2\,\% prior and deciding with the corrected probability), the picture is harsher: sending to everyone loses money in all the structures and not even the best case (low fixed cost) becomes profitable, since the model brings it to break-even ($\approx-1$\,\%, against the $-$83\,\% of sending to everyone) with a minimal fraction of sends. The business reading is that, at realistic click rates, targeting is indispensable but profitability is dictated by the unit economics (cost per email versus CPL).

\section{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Synthetic variables.} The enriched demographic variables (labour status, marital status, household size, rooms and vehicle ownership) are stochastic imputations from aggregate INE distributions; they are not observed at the individual level. An ablation study confirms this quantitatively: a propensity model with \emph{only} product variables matches the full model, whereas one with \emph{only} user variables barely beats chance (PR-AUC 0,278 vs.\ 0,238). This sets a low ceiling on the demographic signal and explains why both the inverse model and the demographic recommender have little discriminative capacity. It is the dominant limitation of the work.

    \item \textbf{Extreme sparsity.} 83,8\% of users have a single event, which prevents the construction of reliable behavioural profiles for the majority of the base and explains why the individual-history component of the recommender adds nothing (weight $\alpha=0$).

    \item \textbf{Reduced catalogue.} With only 25 product categories and clicks concentrated in a few of them, the popularity baseline is very strong, which limits the measurable margin for improvement of any recommender.

    \item \textbf{Size of the \textit{warm} subpopulation.} The \emph{warm} model only contributes for users with a history, who are a minority; for the majority (a single event) the limit of the synthetic demographic signal still applies.

    \item \textbf{Forecasting with a short history.} Prophet has only 22 weeks of activity, which limits the reliability of the forecast beyond a few weeks and produces wide confidence intervals. M4 was re-audited with a rolling \textit{backtest} and the Diebold-Mariano test (with the Harvey-Leybourne-Newbold small-sample correction), but the scarcity of origins (14) limits the statistical power, so the evidence is suggestive.

    \item \textbf{The model captures only one stage of the funnel (click given open).} An email passes through two successive filters: send\,$\rightarrow$\,open\,$\rightarrow$\,click. As the data only contain \emph{opened} emails, the target variable is ``click \emph{given that} it was opened'': the model estimates $P(\text{click}\mid\text{open})$ and does not model the first stage (the probability that the email is opened at all). Consequently, the \textit{prior} correction to 2\,\% and the ``per email sent'' ROI are exact only if the open rate is similar across products; if one product is opened far more than another, its real propensity per send would differ even if its click-given-open were identical. The natural extension is a two-stage model (one for the open and another for the click) whose product, $P(\text{opens})\cdot P(\text{click}\mid\text{opens})$, recovers the real propensity per email sent.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Future work}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Real data instead of imputed data.} The improvement with the greatest potential impact is to replace the synthetic demographic variables with real observations (CRM, behaviour on the advertiser's website). The whole pipeline would perform better with no changes to the modelling.

    \item \textbf{Deepening the \textit{warm} model.} The basic \emph{warm} model has already been implemented and evaluated (improvement demonstrated for users with a history). As an extension, incorporate more behavioural signals (temporal sequence of interactions, recency by sector) and an incremental updating scheme.

    \item \textbf{Improving the temporal forecast.} The re-audit showed that Prophet does not beat naïve baselines on this series. As an alternative, a more exhaustive time-series study would be needed, with proper model-order selection and a comparison of seasonal models (SARIMA), volatility models (GARCH) and neural architectures (LSTM/GRU)~\cite{gallego2024tfg}, as well as incorporating campaign covariates (number of sends, budget) as external regressors. The choice of the moving-average order and of the naïve baseline used in the re-audit was made a priori, without such an analysis; they serve only as reference floors.

    \item \textbf{Evaluation in production and incrementality.} \textit{Offline} validation has known limits in recommendation; a controlled A/B test (with a send group and a control group) would measure the real impact on the click rate and the return on investment, and would allow the \emph{incrementality} (\textit{uplift}) of each send to be estimated, that is, its causal effect, which the ROI analysis does not capture as it measures propensity and not causal effect.
\end{enumerate}
