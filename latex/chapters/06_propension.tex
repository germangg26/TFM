\chapter{Module M2 — Propensity model}
\label{cap:propension}

This chapter develops the click propensity model: first its method (definition, sampling, architecture, algorithm and validation) and then its results (correction of a leak, contribution of the features, comparison of algorithms, calibration, \textit{warm} model and ablation studies). The main metric is PR-AUC, suitable given the strong class imbalance (prevalence $\approx0{,}238$); its interpretation and that of the tests are detailed in Chapter~\ref{cap:evaluacion}.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Problem definition and target variable}

The aim of M2 is to estimate the click propensity of a (user, product) pair: a \textit{score} with which to rank, for each user, which products are most likely to receive a click. The binary target variable is 1 if the event recorded in \texttt{events.csv} is a click and 0 if it is an open. Two ways of estimating that \textit{score} are compared later (a \emph{flat} model and a \emph{hierarchical} sector\,$\rightarrow$\,product one) and, on the empirical evidence, the reasons why the flat one is adopted are justified.

\subsection{Sampling strategy and prior correction}

The event dataset presents a real click rate of $\rho_\text{real} \approx 2\%$ in the full dataset and of $\rho_\text{train} = 23{,}84\%$ in the processed sample (the result of the deduplication and the balanced sampling described in Chapter~\ref{cap:datos}). To allow the model to learn the decision boundary with enough positive examples, it was trained on the balanced dataset. The predicted probabilities are corrected a posteriori by applying the transformation in equation~\ref{eq:prior_correction} with $\rho_\text{real}=0{,}02$ and $\rho_\text{train}=0{,}2384$ (Section~\ref{sec:validacion}).

\subsection{Model architecture}

The \emph{product} features are included because the click is a property of the \emph{pair} (user, product), not of the user in isolation: a single model fed only with user attributes would return the same value for all of that user's products and would therefore be unable to rank them. To discriminate between products there are two options: training a separate model per category (where the product identity is implicit in \emph{which} model is queried) or using a single model that takes the product as an input feature. This second option, besides not fragmenting the data into many small models, can exploit the product attributes (in particular the subject text) and generalise to products with no history. For this reason \textbf{Variant A} (one model per category with \emph{only} user features, the traditional demographic approach) and \textbf{Variant B} (a single model that adds product features) are compared; the comparison in Section~\ref{sec:m2-variables} confirms that adding product features contributes significantly.

On that basis, two architectures are also evaluated. The first is a \textbf{flat model}: a single model estimates $P(\text{click}\mid u,\text{producto})$ directly and its output is used as a \textit{score} to rank each user's products. The second is a \textbf{hierarchical model} in two levels inspired by the purchase decision: a Level 1 estimates the sector propensity $P(\text{click}\mid u,\text{sector})$, a Level 2 the product conditional $P(\text{click}\mid u,\text{sector},\text{producto})$ and the final \textit{score} is the product of the two, $p_\text{sector}\cdot p_\text{producto}$. The comparison of the two architectures (Section~\ref{sec:m2-algos}) shows that the flat model outperforms the hierarchical one across \emph{all} algorithms: the PR-AUC falls by between 0,014 and 0,028 when moving to the hierarchical variant, and the multiplication of the two probabilities, besides adding no signal, decalibrates the predictions (the \textit{Brier} score worsens from $\approx0{,}17$ to $\approx0{,}21$). The reason is that chaining two imperfect estimates accumulates their error instead of cancelling it, and a single flat model can already use the sector as just another feature without paying that cost. The flat model is therefore adopted. The hierarchical structure is retained as an interpretable variant (it decomposes the decision into sector and product), but it is not adopted as the final model.

\subsection{Algorithm and features}

The algorithm selected is \textbf{LightGBM} \cite{ke2017lightgbm}, chosen after also comparing a neural network (multilayer perceptron), HistGradientBoosting, Random Forest and logistic regression on the same partitions; the detailed comparison (Section~\ref{sec:m2-algos}) shows small differences between the tree-based methods (with LightGBM and Random Forest in the lead and the neural network last), which confirms that the performance ceiling is set by the data, not the algorithm. The hyperparameters are given in Table~\ref{tab:lgbm_params}; \texttt{num\_leaves} was reduced to 15 because, with weak signal, a more regularised model generalises better.

The input features combine two groups. The \textbf{user features} (11): age \emph{binned} into groups (\texttt{age\_cat}), gender, labour status, marital status, vehicle ownership, household size, number of rooms, ordinal IPA, municipality type, ordinal distance and demographic segment (M0). The \textbf{product features} (Variant B): category, sector, CPL, marketing attributes (urgency, price sensitivity, perceived risk\ldots) aggregated at sector level (the join by brand covered only 26,7\,\% of events) and the subject text \textit{embeddings}. The latter are obtained by encoding \texttt{subject\_clean} with a multilingual \textit{sentence-transformers} model (384 dimensions) and reducing it to 16 via PCA; they capture \emph{what the email says}, which is what drives the click. To avoid leakage, that PCA is fitted within each cross-validation \textit{fold} (only with the \textit{train} products), not globally. For the same reason the few missing numeric values are not imputed: LightGBM handles \texttt{NaN} natively.

\subsection{Validation}

The evaluation is carried out with \textbf{user-grouped cross-validation} (\texttt{Stratified\-Group\-KFold}, $k=5$; Section~\ref{sec:validacion}). This is a critical point: since all the user features are identical across all of a user's events, validation by event would leave the same user in training and validation, allowing the model to memorise and inflating the metric. Grouping by \texttt{id\_user} measures generalisation to \emph{unseen} users, which is the real cold-start scenario. The main metric is \mbox{PR-AUC}, with \mbox{AUC-ROC} as support. The comparisons are contrasted with 95\,\% \textit{bootstrap} confidence intervals, paired differences, the per-fold Wilcoxon test and the Holm correction.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Correction of a data leakage in the validation}

The initial validation used \texttt{StratifiedKFold} \emph{by event}, which reintroduced the leakage described in Section~\ref{sec:validacion}: the same user appeared at once in training and validation with an identical \textit{feature} vector, producing an optimistic metric. On replacing it with \texttt{StratifiedGroupKFold} \emph{grouping by} \texttt{id\_user}, the AUC-ROC falls from 0,695 to 0,563 (Figure~\ref{fig:m2_leakage}). The value of 0,695 was therefore a product of the leakage; the 0,563 is the realistic estimate of the predictive power of the demographic features on their own.

The subject text \textit{embeddings} are a central piece of that product signal.

\label{sec:m2-algos}

With the features fixed, five algorithms are compared (a multilayer perceptron neural network, LightGBM, HistGradientBoosting, Random Forest and logistic regression) under the two architectures: the \emph{flat} model (a single model estimates $P(\text{click}\mid u,\text{producto})$) and the \emph{hierarchical} one ($p_\text{sector}\cdot p_\text{producto}$), all on the same user-grouped GroupKFold partitions. Three conclusions can be drawn. First, the flat architecture outperforms the hierarchical one across the five algorithms ($\Delta$PR-AUC between $-0{,}014$ and $-0{,}028$): multiplying by the sector probability adds error and decalibrates the probabilities (the \textit{Brier} score worsens from $\approx0{,}17$ to $\approx0{,}21$); the flat model is therefore adopted. Second, the neural network comes last in both architectures, a result consistent with the recent evidence that, on tabular data, tree-based methods outperform deep learning~\cite{grinsztajn2022}; here, with imbalanced classes and weak signal, a more complex model is not justified. Third, the differences between the tree methods are small (Random Forest and LightGBM are tied within the confidence interval); LightGBM is kept for its native handling of categorical features and missing values and for being the industry standard. No algorithm breaks away from the PR-AUC $0{,}40$ region: the ceiling is set by the data, not the algorithm.

To rule out that the comparison penalised any algorithm for using its default configuration (in particular the neural network), the hyperparameters of each model were tuned by a bounded grid search over the \emph{same} GroupKFold partitions and the flat model. After tuning, the order is maintained: Random Forest $0{,}411$ and LightGBM $0{,}409$ remain tied in the lead, HistGradientBoosting $0{,}406$, and the neural network $0{,}395$, despite improving on its default configuration, remains below the trees, followed by logistic regression $0{,}392$. Tuning therefore alters none of the three conclusions nor the adopted model: the advantage of the trees over the neural network is structural and not an effect of the parameterisation.

\subsection{Interpretability}

Figure~\ref{fig:m2_importance} shows the feature importance of the adopted model (with product features). The most relevant are \texttt{ipa\_class} (purchasing power), \texttt{age\_cat} (binned age) and \texttt{demo\_cluster} (M0 demographic cluster), which takes third position: the cluster works as a compact summary of the demographic profile. That said, the importance by number of \textit{splits} reflects how much the model \emph{uses} a feature, not its real predictive value: the ablation study (Table~\ref{tab:m2_ablation}) shows that dispensing with the cluster (and with the rest of the user features) does not reduce the PR-AUC, so that its role is operational (solving the cold start) rather than predictive.

\subsection{Prior correction and sensitivity}

The predictions are calibrated to the real production prior ($\rho_\text{real}=0{,}02$) by applying the transformation of Dal Pozzolo et al.~\cite{dalpozzolo2015} on the \textit{log-odds} scale, whose properties (monotonicity and ranking invariance) are justified in Chapter~\ref{cap:evaluacion}. After the correction, the probabilities go from a mean of 0,227 (balanced training scale) to 0,022, directly interpretable as estimates of real behaviour (consistent with the $\approx 2$\,\% real click rate).

\paragraph{Sensitivity to the real prior.} The value $\rho_\text{real}=0{,}02$ is an estimate; the real click rate could lie around 1\,\% or 3\,\%. To check the robustness of the conclusions, the correction was repeated with those three priors (Table~\ref{tab:m2_prior}). By the monotonicity of the transformation, the ranking power is invariant: AUC-ROC ($0{,}684$) and PR-AUC ($0{,}402$) do not change in any of the three scenarios. The only thing that shifts is the absolute level of the probabilities, whose mean follows the assumed prior ($1{,}1$\,\% / $2{,}2$\,\% / $3{,}2$\,\%). In other words, the uncertainty about the prior affects the \emph{scale} of the probabilities but not the \emph{quality} of the model nor the ranking of users, which is what is exploited in the prioritisation of sends. The production-scale ROI analysis (Chapter~\ref{cap:negocio}) does use these corrected probabilities.

\paragraph{Calibration.} Beyond the order, it is of interest that the probabilities be realistic. The \textit{Brier score} (lower is better) of the flat model on the training scale is 0,167, and the reliability curve shows no large systematic deviations from the diagonal, which indicates a reasonable calibration. After the \textit{prior} correction, the probabilities are on the production scale ($\approx2\%$), so their \textit{Brier} is no longer comparable with that of the balanced scale.

\subsection{\textit{Warm} model for users with history}

The M2 described is \emph{cold}: it uses only demographics and is valid for any user, including new ones. For users \emph{with history}, a \emph{warm} model was evaluated that adds \textbf{behaviour} features (number of events, clicks, \textit{click-rate}, sectors and recency), computed \emph{only with training data} to avoid temporal leakage. So that the comparison does not depend on a single arbitrary temporal cut, it is evaluated with temporal cross-validation (expanding-window \textit{TimeSeriesSplit}, 5 successive past$\to$future cuts), measuring \emph{cold} and \emph{warm} on the test events of users with history at each cut. The \emph{warm} model substantially improves on the \emph{cold} one consistently across the five cuts: PR-AUC $0{,}827\pm0{,}031 \to 0{,}943\pm0{,}012$ and AUC-ROC $0{,}810\pm0{,}033 \to 0{,}941\pm0{,}018$ (mean\,$\pm$\,standard deviation across folds), with a mean improvement $\Delta$PR-AUC $=+0{,}116\pm0{,}031$ and positive sign in 5 out of 5 cuts. The stability across folds (small deviation) reinforces that the gain is real and not an artefact of a particular cut. This gain applies only to the minority of users with history; for new users the \emph{cold} model is kept. These absolute values are not comparable with the global PR-AUC of M2 (0,402): they are measured on an intrinsically easier subpopulation (recurrent users, with \textit{click-rate} around 0,5 versus 0,238 globally), so what is informative is the \emph{relative improvement} of the \emph{warm} model over the \emph{cold} one ($\Delta\approx+0{,}12$), not the figures themselves. In the deployment, a \texttt{/propensity} \textit{endpoint} automatically selects \emph{warm} or \emph{cold} depending on whether or not the user has history.

\subsection{Ablation: which group of features contributes?}

To quantify the real contribution of each group of features, an \textbf{ablation study} was carried out: the model is retrained (in its flat form, with \textit{out-of-fold} prediction and user-grouped GroupKFold validation) removing groups of features and measuring the change in PR-AUC with \textit{bootstrap} confidence interval. Table~\ref{tab:m2_ablation} gives the results. A distinction is made between the \emph{randomly sampled synthetic} features (labour status, marital status, vehicle, household, rooms), the \emph{real user signal} (real age and geographic features derived from the postal code) and the \emph{product} features.

The results are clear: (i) removing the five randomly sampled synthetic features does not change the metric (not significant difference, $p=0{,}68$): they add no signal; (ii) the best numerical model keeps \emph{only} the real user signal (age and geography) plus the product, although its advantage over the full model is also not significant ($p=0{,}06$); (iii) a model with only product features matches the full model (not significant difference, $p=0{,}07$), whereas one with only user features barely beats chance (PR-AUC 0,278 vs.\ 0,238; AUC-ROC 0,56). The synthetic demographic features add no predictive power: all the signal resides in the product content (sector, category and subject text). This is the quantitative confirmation of the limitation anticipated in Chapter~\ref{cap:datos}.

\subsection{Does the model beat the click rate by category?}

If all the signal is in the product, the truly demanding \textit{baseline} is not popularity, but assigning to each pair the historical click rate of the product's category (a frequency table). It is evaluated \textit{out-of-fold} (the rate is estimated only with the \textit{train} of each \textit{fold}, user-grouped GroupKFold) and compared with the flat model (Table~\ref{tab:m2_ctr}). The model significantly outperforms that frequency table: PR-AUC 0,402 versus 0,316 ($\Delta=+0{,}086$, 95\% CI $[+0{,}078,+0{,}094]$, $p\approx0$). The improvement is not residual: it confirms that the model does not merely memorise the average propensity of each category, but exploits the specific subject text (via \textit{embeddings}) to discriminate between products of the same category. This is the evidence that supports \textbf{H1}. The email subject is a \emph{legitimate} feature, available at the moment of deciding the send, so using it does not constitute leakage; and whether the advantage comes from the semantics of the text or from a finer encoding of the category itself does not alter the operational conclusion: incorporating the subject improves the \textit{ranking} of sends relative to working only with the aggregated category.

\section{Inverse model: product \texorpdfstring{$\rightarrow$}{->} profile}

An inverse model was built that, given a product, identifies the user profiles with the highest click probability. Methodologically, the M2 \emph{forward} model is queried by fixing the product and enumerating all combinations of a subset of features (age, sex, income, vehicle, municipality type; 720 combinations), from which the marginal probabilities per feature are derived. The effect of the profile turns out to be weak: the marginal click probability per age group varies only between 0,06 and 0,14 depending on the product, which indicates that it is the product, and not the demographic profile, that mostly determines the propensity. The method is valid, but its informative capacity is limited by the synthetic nature of the user features.
