\chapter{System design and evaluation framework}
\label{cap:evaluacion}

This chapter presents an overview of the pipeline and, above all, the evaluation framework common to all modules: which metrics are used and how to interpret them, how validation is carried out without data leakage, and which statistical tests support each comparison. Gathering these definitions here avoids repeating them in every module and allows the following chapters to focus on their results.

\section{Pipeline overview}

The system is organised as a sequential pipeline that transforms raw campaign data into actionable predictions and recommendations, grouped into four functional blocks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Block I — Data preparation} (\texttt{01\_clean}, \texttt{02\_geo}, \texttt{03\_users}): cleaning, construction of dimensional tables and demographic enrichment (Chapter~\ref{cap:datos}).
    \item \emph{Block II — Segmentation} (\texttt{05\_demo\_segmentation}, \texttt{06\_segmentation}): two levels of user grouping, M0 (demographic) and M1 (behavioural) (Chapter~\ref{cap:segmentacion}).
    \item \emph{Block III — Predictive modelling} (\texttt{07\_propensity}, \texttt{08\_recommender}): click propensity (M2, Chapter~\ref{cap:propension}) and cluster-based recommender (M3, Chapter~\ref{cap:recomendador}).
    \item \emph{Block IV — Temporal forecasting} (\texttt{09\_forecast}): prediction of the weekly click volume (M4, Chapter~\ref{cap:forecast}).
\end{itemize}
Complementary analyses include a stability diagnostic for the number of clusters (\texttt{05b\_k\_stability}), an inverse product\,$\rightarrow$\,profile model (\texttt{10\_inverse\_profile}) and a deployment example with a return-on-investment analysis (Chapter~\ref{cap:negocio}). Each module chapter presents its \emph{method} and its \emph{results} together, to ease reading.

\section{Evaluation metrics}
\label{sec:metricas}

Each type of problem requires different metrics. The metrics used are defined below and, most importantly, how to interpret them: which value is good and which is the reference or ``chance'' value.

\subsection{Click propensity (binary classification)}

The problem involves imbalanced classes (few clicks against many non-interactions), which conditions the choice of metrics: accuracy is misleading, because a model predicting ``never click'' would be right most of the time without being useful.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{PR-AUC} (area under the precision-recall curve), the main metric. It summarises how well the model identifies the rare class (the click) across different thresholds. It ranges from 0 to 1; higher is better. Its \emph{reference} value (what chance would yield) is not 0,5 but the prevalence of positives: here $\approx0{,}238$. This is why a PR-AUC of 0,40 over a prevalence of 0,24 does indicate genuine signal. It is preferred over AUC-ROC under imbalance because it focuses on the positive class~\cite{davisgoadrich2006,saito2015}.
    \item \textbf{AUC-ROC} (area under the ROC curve)~\cite{fawcett2006}, a supporting metric. It measures the ability to \emph{rank} a randomly chosen positive above a randomly chosen negative. It ranges from 0 to 1: 0,5 is chance, 1 is perfect. As a rough guide, $\approx0{,}7$ is modest, $0{,}8$ good and $\geq0{,}9$ excellent. Under imbalance it tends to be \emph{optimistic}, which is why the main metric is PR-AUC.
    \item \textbf{Brier score}~\cite{brier1950}, the quality of the \emph{calibration}. It measures whether the probabilities are \emph{realistic} (not only whether the ordering is correct): it is the mean squared error between the predicted probability and the actual outcome (0/1). Lower is better (0 = perfect).
    \item \textbf{F1, MCC and Balanced Accuracy}, the quality of the binary decision at a fixed threshold (here, the prevalence 0,238). The F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall (0 to 1). The MCC (Matthews correlation coefficient~\cite{matthews1975}) summarises the full confusion matrix and is robust to imbalance: it ranges from $-1$ to $1$, with 0 = chance. The Balanced Accuracy averages the accuracy on each class separately (0,5 = chance).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Recommendation (ranking quality)}

Here the goal is not to predict a number but an \emph{ordering} of categories; the metrics measure whether the categories relevant to the user appear at the top of a list of size $K$.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hit Rate@$K$} (HR@$K$): the fraction of users for whom \emph{at least one} relevant category is among the $K$ recommended. It answers ``did I get anything right in the top-$K$?''. It ranges from 0 to 1.
    \item \textbf{NDCG@$K$} (Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain)~\cite{jarvelin2002}: like HR but rewarding the ordering, giving more weight to correct hits in the first positions. From 0 to 1.
    \item MAP@$K$ (Mean Average Precision): the average precision along the list, also sensitive to ordering. From 0 to 1.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Temporal forecasting}

For the weekly click volume, prediction error metrics are used (lower is better):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{MAE} (mean absolute error) and \textbf{RMSE} (root mean squared error): in the same units as the series (clicks/week); the RMSE penalises large errors more heavily.
    \item MAPE (mean absolute percentage error): the relative error in \%; convenient to interpret but unstable when the actual values are small.
\end{itemize}

\section{Validation without data leakage}
\label{sec:validacion}

A reliable estimate of performance requires avoiding data leakage: the model accessing during training information that it would not have in production, which artificially inflates the metrics~\cite{kaufman2012leakage}. Three schemes are used depending on the module:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{User-grouped cross-validation} (\texttt{GroupKFold}, M2): it splits the data into 5 \emph{folds}, guaranteeing that no user is at once in training and validation. It is essential here because a user's features are identical across all their events; without grouping, the model would ``memorise'' the user and overestimate its ability. It measures generalisation to \emph{new} users.
    \item \emph{Temporal cross-validation} (\texttt{TimeSeriesSplit}, the \textit{warm} model and M4): training is always \emph{past} and validation \emph{future}, respecting the chronological order.
    \item \emph{Reconstruction using only \textit{train}} (M3): each user's segment is recomputed using only the training data, so that it does not ``know'' the test period.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Prior-shift correction.} The propensity model is trained on a \emph{balanced} sample (\textasciitilde24\,\% clicks) but in production the real rate is $\approx 2$\,\%; without adjustment, the probabilities would come out inflated. The transformation of Dal Pozzolo et al.~\cite{dalpozzolo2015} (equation~\ref{eq:prior_correction}) rescales them to the real prior. It is monotonic: it changes the magnitude of the probabilities but \emph{not} their ordering, and therefore does not affect the ranking metrics (PR-AUC, AUC-ROC).

\paragraph{Cross-validation versus \textit{bootstrap}.} They are complementary and answer different questions. Cross-validation measures \emph{how well the model generalises} (it retrains $k$ times, each data point being test once). The \textit{bootstrap} measures the \emph{uncertainty} of an already computed metric: it resamples the test set at random many times (with replacement) and observes how much the metric varies, which gives a confidence interval without needing to retrain.

\section{Statistical testing}
\label{sec:tests}

That one model obtains a slightly better metric than another is \emph{not} enough to claim it is superior: the difference could be due to the chance of the particular sample. Statistical tests quantify that doubt. The common idea is the \emph{$p$-value}: the probability of observing a difference as large as the one measured \emph{if there were in fact no difference}; a $p<0{,}05$ is interpreted as evidence that the difference is not coincidental. The use of non-parametric tests to compare models follows the recommendations of Dem\v{s}ar~\cite{demsar2006}. The tests used are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bootstrap confidence interval}~\cite{efron1979}: the test set is resampled with replacement (1.000 times) and the metric or its difference is recomputed. If the 95\,\% interval of a difference \emph{does not include 0}, the difference is robust. It does not depend on the number of \emph{folds}.
    \item \textbf{Wilcoxon test (paired)}~\cite{wilcoxon1945}: it compares two models \emph{fold by fold}, without assuming normality. An important \emph{limitation}: with 5 \emph{folds} the smallest possible $p$-value is 0,0625, so a non-significant result may be due to a lack of power rather than a lack of difference; this is why it is supported by the \textit{bootstrap}.
    \item \textbf{Friedman test}~\cite{friedman1937}: a \emph{global} test to compare three or more methods over the same subjects (``is there any difference between them?''). It is performed before comparing pairs.
    \item \textbf{McNemar test}~\cite{mcnemar1947}: it compares two methods \emph{case by case} on success/failure, focusing only on the cases where they disagree. Useful for contrasting the success rate (HR@5) between the recommender and a \textit{baseline}.
    \item \textbf{Holm correction}~\cite{holm1979}: when performing \emph{several} comparisons at once, the probability of a false positive accumulates. Holm tightens the significance threshold sequentially (requiring the strictest bar of the smallest $p$), controlling that risk.
    \item \textbf{Diebold-Mariano (DM) test}~\cite{diebold1995}: the equivalent for comparing \emph{time series predictions}; it contrasts the week-by-week errors of two \textit{forecast} models. With small samples the Harvey-Leybourne-Newbold correction~\cite{harvey1997} is applied (a small-sample factor and Student's $t$), essential here given the few available origins.
    \item \emph{Effect sizes}: the $p$-value tells whether a difference exists, not \emph{how large} it is. We report Kendall's W (for the Friedman; 0 = no effect, 1 = maximum effect) and the rank-biserial correlation $|r|$ (for the paired Wilcoxon tests; as a rough guide, $0{,}1$ small, $0{,}3$ medium, $0{,}5$ large).
\end{itemize}

All random seeds are fixed at 42 to guarantee reproducibility (Appendix~\ref{ap:reproducibilidad}).
