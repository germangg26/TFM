\chapter{Module M4 — Temporal forecasting}
\label{cap:forecast}

\section{Method}

Module M4 applies Prophet \cite{taylor2018forecasting} to the time series of weekly aggregated clicks. The dataset spans 52 weeks, but the first 30 show virtually no activity (0--50 clicks), as the campaign had not yet started. Including this inactive period in training leads the model to learn a downward trend towards zero. To avoid this, the model is trained exclusively on the \textbf{active period}: the 22 weeks from the first one with at least 50 clicks (the week of 28 July 2025), with a range of 108 to 1.759 clicks.

Given that the data are aggregated by week (not by day), Prophet's weekly seasonality provides no signal and is disabled. Annual seasonality is likewise not estimable with fewer than six months of activity and is also disabled. The model is configured with a linear trend, \texttt{changepoint\_prior\_scale}\,=\,0{,}5 to capture the October peak and the subsequent drop, and \texttt{n\_changepoints}\,=\,10. The confidence interval is 90\,\%. For evaluation we first use a hold-out of the last 4 weeks and, above all, a rolling backtest that is compared against naïve baselines by means of the Diebold-Mariano test (Section~\ref{sec:tests}).

\section{Results}

On the hold-out of the last 4 weeks, Prophet obtains MAE\,=\,447 clicks/week, RMSE\,=\,507,7 and MAPE\,=\,73,3\,\%, and predicts 259--452 clicks/week for January--February 2026 (Figure~\ref{fig:forecast}). The MAPE is conditioned by an atypical peak in the first week of the hold-out; the other three weeks show errors of 2--29\,\%. The width of the confidence intervals reflects the uncertainty of an active historical record of only 22 weeks.

\subsection{Re-audit with baselines}

The 4-week hold-out is too short for a reliable comparison. A \textbf{one-step rolling
backtest} (14 origins) was carried out: for each week the model is trained on everything prior and predicts the
following one, comparing Prophet with two naïve baselines (\emph{naïve}: next week = the last one;
and moving average of 3). Table~\ref{tab:m4_backtest} shows the result.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{M4: error of the one-step rolling backtest (14 origins)}
\label{tab:m4_backtest}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{MAE} & \textbf{RMSE} \\
\midrule
Prophet        & 749,7 & 874,7 \\
\textbf{Naïve} & \textbf{502,3} & \textbf{604,6} \\
Moving average 3  & 583,7 & 703,7 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}


The result is clear in terms of point error: on this short and volatile series, \textbf{the naïve baselines beat
Prophet} (MAE 750 against 502 for the \emph{naïve}). That said, with only 14 origins the Diebold-Mariano
test requires the \textbf{Harvey-Leybourne-Newbold correction} (small-sample factor and Student's $t$
with $n-1$ degrees of freedom); once applied, the difference turns out to be \textbf{marginal} (vs.\ \emph{naïve}
$p=0{,}059$; vs.\ moving average $p=0{,}073$): it does not reach significance at 5\,\%, which is consistent with such a short
historical record. The fair reading is that the simple ``next week will be like the last one'' matches or
surpasses Prophet ---whose trend flexibility overfits with so few data---, although the evidence is
\emph{suggestive} rather than conclusive owing to the scarcity of points.

It should be stressed that these two baselines are deliberately simple reference points, not optimised models: the naïve forecast (the last observed value) and a moving average whose order (three weeks) was fixed \emph{a priori}, without a model-order selection analysis. Their role here is only to provide a hard floor against which to judge Prophet, not to propose a final forecasting model. A rigorous forecasting study would require a more exhaustive comparison ---with proper order selection (e.g.\ via the ACF/PACF), seasonal models (SARIMA), volatility models (GARCH) and neural architectures (LSTM/GRU)--- which exceeds the scope of this module and is left as future work.
