\chapter{State of the art}
\label{cap:estado_arte}

\section{Email marketing and user behaviour}

Email marketing is one of the longest-standing direct marketing channels in the digital environment, with an average return of between 36 and 40 dollars for every dollar invested according to the industry's annual reports \cite{litmus2023}. The literature agrees that this performance depends on the ability to segment the audience and personalise the message, two problems on which the remainder of this chapter rests.

User behaviour in response to an email is usually modelled by means of three types of event: impression (\textit{open}), active interaction (\textit{click}) and absence of response (\textit{ignored}). The click rate over impressions (\textit{click-through rate}, CTR) varies between 1\% and 5\% depending on the sector, which produces a strong class imbalance that conditions the methodological decisions in predictive models \cite{chaffey2024}.

From the predictive standpoint, the earliest works in this domain were based on logistic models and decision trees applied to attributes of the email and the recipient \cite{sahni2016}. With the emergence of \textit{gradient boosting} methods and the increase in data availability, approaches based on LightGBM \cite{ke2017lightgbm} and XGBoost \cite{chen2016xgboost} have become the industrial standard for behaviour prediction in direct marketing, thanks to their ability to handle categorical variables, missing data and class imbalance natively.

\section{Customer segmentation}

Customer segmentation aims to identify groups of users with similar behavioural patterns or demographic characteristics, so that communication can be tailored to each group. The most widely used unsupervised clustering methods in this domain are KMeans \cite{macqueen1967}, Gaussian mixture models \cite{reynolds2009} and density-based clustering algorithms such as DBSCAN \cite{ester1996} and its hierarchical extension HDBSCAN \cite{campello2013}.

The choice of the number of clusters is one of the fundamental problems of unsupervised learning. The most widely used criteria are the silhouette coefficient \cite{rousseeuw1987}, the Calinski-Harabasz index \cite{calinski1974} and the Davies-Bouldin index \cite{davies1979}, which assess intra-cluster cohesion and inter-cluster separation from complementary perspectives.

In high-dimensional settings, it is common to apply dimensionality reduction techniques prior to clustering. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) \cite{jolliffe2002} is the most widely used linear technique; autoencoders \cite{hinton2006} make it possible to capture non-linear relationships that PCA does not model. Works on \textit{deep embedded clustering} show that representations learnt by neural networks can improve clustering relative to linear methods by capturing non-linear structure in the data \cite{xie2016}.

For the visualisation of clusters in two dimensions, UMAP \cite{mcinnes2018} has progressively replaced t-SNE \cite{vandermaaten2008} in practice thanks to its greater speed and to its preservation of the global structure of the data.

\section{Predictive models in marketing}

\subsection{Propensity modelling}

Propensity modelling (\textit{propensity modeling}) consists of estimating the probability that an individual will perform an action of interest ---in this case, clicking on an email--- given their observable profile. It is a direct application of binary classification with particular emphasis on the calibration of probabilities.

A frequent challenge in this context is sampling bias: when the training data are built with an artificial proportion of positives and negatives that differs from the real distribution, the predicted probabilities are inflated or deflated with respect to the true prior. The correction of Dal Pozzolo et al. \cite{dalpozzolo2015} provides an exact log-odds adjustment to recover probabilities calibrated with respect to the real prior:
\begin{equation}
    \text{logit}(\hat{p}_\text{real}) = \text{logit}(\hat{p}_\text{model}) + \log\frac{\rho_\text{real}}{1-\rho_\text{real}} - \log\frac{\rho_\text{train}}{1-\rho_\text{train}}
    \label{eq:prior_correction}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_\text{real}$ is the real positive rate in production and $\rho_\text{train}$ is the rate in the training set.

\subsection{Representation of textual content}

The subject of an email is a short text whose semantics directly influences the click decision, so incorporating it as a predictive variable requires converting it into a numerical representation. Language models based on \textit{Transformers}, such as BERT \cite{devlin2019bert}, learn contextual representations of language from large corpora. Building on this, Sentence-BERT \cite{reimers2019sbert} produces sentence \textit{embeddings} comparable by means of cosine similarity, and its multilingual extension through knowledge distillation \cite{reimers2020multilingual} makes it possible to obtain consistent representations for Spanish texts. These \textit{embeddings} can be used as input variables in tabular models, providing the content signal that the product's categorical variables do not capture.

\subsection{Recommender systems}

Recommender systems are usually classified into three paradigms: collaborative filtering, content-based filtering and hybrids \cite{burke2002}. In settings with high sparsity of interactions ---common in email marketing, where most users have only one or two observed interactions--- classic collaborative filtering based on matrix factorisation \cite{koren2009} loses effectiveness, and hybrid approaches that combine user profile information with the behavioural patterns of their segment are preferred.

\subsection{Temporal demand forecasting}

Forecasting the volume of clicks at an aggregate level is a univariate time series problem. Prophet \cite{taylor2018forecasting} is an additive model that decomposes the series into trend, seasonality and holiday effects, with particular robustness to missing data and trend changes. It has shown competitive performance in demand problems with multiple seasonality when the available history covers at least two complete seasonal periods.

\subsection{Rigorous validation and model comparison}

Reliable estimation of a model's performance requires avoiding data leakage (\textit{data leakage}) \cite{kaufman2012leakage}, a problem that is developed in Chapter~\ref{cap:evaluacion}. A common case arises when several observations share the same entity ---for example, multiple events of a single user with identical attributes---: a cross-validation that does not group by entity allows the model to memorise profiles and overestimate its generalisation ability, so grouped cross-validation must be used. Moreover, the mere difference in a metric between two models is not conclusive: to support the claim that one model outperforms another, statistical tests are recommended, such as Dietterich's paired $5\times2$cv test \cite{dietterich1998} or the non-parametric tests (Wilcoxon and Friedman) systematised by Dem\v{s}ar \cite{demsar2006}, together with corrections for multiple comparisons when several models are contrasted at the same time.

\section{Geographic and demographic data enrichment}

The enrichment of user profiles with geographic and demographic information is a well-established practice in precision marketing. In the Spanish context, the National Statistics Institute (INE) publishes data on economic activity, unemployment and household composition at the municipal and provincial level, which make it possible to impute socioeconomic characteristics for users of whom only the postal code is available \cite{ine2025}.

This ecological imputation strategy ---assigning to each individual the distribution of their area of residence--- introduces an approximation that may not exactly reflect individual characteristics, especially in municipalities with high internal heterogeneity. Nevertheless, in the absence of observed individual data, it constitutes the most rigorous and traceable alternative available \cite{greenland2001}.
